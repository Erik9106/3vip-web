How to register a patent in Peru: requirements, costs and timelines

What you need to register a patent, how much it costs to patent a product and how long it takes. Patenting has two costs: the official fees paid to INDECOPI, at two different stages of the procedure, and the professional fees of whoever drafts and prosecutes the application. Here we explain the requirements, how much is paid, when it is paid and how long each stage takes, with the time limits set by Andean Community Decision 486.

First things first: patent or trademark?

Many people search for “patenting a product” when what they actually need is to register their trademark. They are different forms of protection, with different costs and timelines. It pays to be clear on this before paying any fee.

I don't want anyone copying my product

If your product solves a technical problem in a new way (a mechanism, a tool, a formula, a device), what is protected is the invention, and the route is a patent. It stops others from making or selling that solution, whatever they call it. That is the procedure this page explains.

I don't want anyone using my name or logo

If the product is not technically new, but you want nobody else selling under your name or logo, what you need is a trademark. The exclusive right to a trademark is acquired through registration (art. 154). See what it costs to register a trademark →

A new product may need both: the patent protects how it works, and the trademark protects the name it is known by in the market.

Requirements to register a patent

There are two kinds of requirements: those the invention must meet, which the examiner assesses in the substantive examination, and the documents the application must contain. The articles cited are from Decision 486.

What the invention must meet

Invention patent (art. 14):

  • Novelty: it must not have been made available to the public, by written or oral description, use, sale or any other means, before the filing date (art. 16).
  • Inventive step: it must not be obvious to a person of ordinary skill in the relevant technical field (art. 18).
  • Industrial application: it must be capable of being made or used in any kind of industry, including services (art. 19).

Utility model: novelty and technical advantage, that is, a new shape, configuration or arrangement of elements that gives the object a utility, advantage or technical effect it did not have before (art. 81).

In both cases, the description must be clear and complete enough for a skilled person to carry out the invention (art. 28), and the claims must be clear, concise and fully supported by the description (art. 30).

What documents the application includes

Under article 26, the application contains:

  • the request, on a form, with the details of the applicant, the inventor and the title of the invention (art. 27);
  • the description of the invention;
  • one or more claims, which define what is protected;
  • the drawings, where needed to understand it;
  • the abstract;
  • the powers of attorney, if a representative acts;
  • proof of payment of the fee.

Where applicable: the access contract if the invention was developed from genetic resources, the license to use traditional knowledge, the biological material deposit certificate and the document assigning the right from the inventor to the applicant.

Have you already shown or sold your product? A disclosure made by the inventor, or by a third party who obtained the information from the inventor, does not destroy novelty if it took place within the year before the application was filed (art. 17). After that year, your own disclosure can prevent the patent. If you have already disclosed it, file as soon as possible.

Annuities: the cost that comes later

An invention patent carries one more cost that many people do not anticipate: to keep the pending application, and later the granted patent, in force, an annual fee must be paid. Utility models do not have this payment.

The first annuity is paid on the first anniversary of the filing date. Each annuity is paid in advance and falls due on the last day of the month in which the application was filed: if it was filed on September 15, it falls due every year on September 30. Two or more annuities may be paid in advance. If the due date is missed, there is a six-month grace period to pay with a surcharge, during which the patent or application remains fully in force. If it is not paid within that period either, the patent or application lapses by operation of law (art. 80).

Before filing: the prior art search

A patent is only granted if the invention is new. Knowing what already exists before drafting the application avoids paying fees for something that is already patented or published, and makes it possible to write claims that stand apart from it.

The procedure, step by step

This is how an application proceeds when nothing goes wrong. Invention patents and utility models follow the same stages, but for utility models time limits are halved, except publication, which takes place at 12 months (art. 85). Each stage shows both time limits. Unless another law is named, the articles cited are from Andean Community Decision 486.

  1. Filing the application

    The application is filed with INDECOPI's Directorate of Inventions and New Technologies and the filing fee is paid: S/ 396.00 for an invention or S/ 226.80 for a utility model. For the application to receive a filing date, which is what establishes priority over third parties, it must contain at least (art. 33):

    • an indication that a patent is being requested;
    • the applicant's details, or details that allow the office to contact them;
    • the description of the invention;
    • the drawings, where relevant;
    • proof of payment of the fee.

    If any of these elements is missing, the application is not admitted for processing and receives no filing date.

  2. Formal examination

    The office checks that all documents required for the procedure are present (art. 38). In practice it usually responds within about two weeks, stating whether the application passed or has formal objections and giving the file number.

    If there are objections, the applicant may correct them within the time limit, which can be extended once, on request, for an equal period (art. 39). If they are not corrected, the application is deemed abandoned.

    Invention: 30 working days · Utility model: 15 working days
    Corrections — Invention: 2 months + 2 · Utility model: 1 month + 1

  3. Publication

    Once the period from the filing date (or the claimed priority date) has elapsed, the file becomes public and the office orders its publication in INDECOPI's electronic Industrial Property Gazette. After the formal examination is complete, the applicant may request earlier publication (arts. 40 and 85).

    Invention: 18 months · Utility model: 12 months · Or earlier, on request

  4. Requesting and paying for substantive examination

    After publication, the office notifies the applicant to request substantive examination and pay the balance of the fee: S/ 324.00 for an invention patent or S/ 97.20 for a utility model. The request is filed with proof of payment within the time limit counted from publication. If it is not filed, the application is deemed abandoned (art. 44).

    Invention: 6 months · Utility model: 3 months · If not requested: abandonment

  5. Substantive examination and decision

    The file waits its turn for examination. The examiner issues a patentability report, which is notified to the applicant. If it is favorable, no response is needed. If it is adverse, the applicant must respond within the time limit counted from notification, which can be extended once on request (art. 45). The response must overcome every objection raised by the examiner.

    The procedure ends with a decision that grants the patent in full, grants it in part or refuses it.

    Response — Invention: 60 working days + 30 · Utility model: 30 + 15

How time limits are counted. When Decision 486 sets a time limit in days, they are working days; when it sets one in months, it runs from date to date (art. 5). Time limits triggered by a notification or publication start running on the day after that notification or publication, unless otherwise provided (art. 4).

Once granted, an invention patent lasts 20 years (art. 50) and a utility model 10 years (art. 84), counted from the filing date. That is why prosecution time is not added to the protection: it runs within it.

When the procedure gets complicated

Everything above describes a trouble-free procedure. These are the special cases that call for a strategy of their own.

Third-party opposition

Within the 60 working days following publication, anyone with a legitimate interest may oppose the application (art. 42). The opposition must be answered with technical and legal arguments.

Adverse substantive examination

An adverse report is not the end of the procedure. With a well-built technical response, objections can be overcome: in our files, 12 of 12 rescues ended in grant. See patent rescue →

Unreasonable delays

If the office delays the procedure unreasonably, a patent term adjustment can be requested under articles 32 and 33 of Legislative Decree No. 1075.

Refusal or abandonment

A request for reconsideration or an appeal can be filed against the decision within 15 working days counted from the day after notification; once that period has passed, nothing more can be done. If the application was deemed abandoned, a divisional application may be considered so as not to lose everything. And once administrative remedies are exhausted, judicial review before the courts remains available.
